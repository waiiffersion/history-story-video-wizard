# Turning history into a story people can hear

## The burden question

Start with a universal pressure that can be understood before names and dates:

- How does a new ruler prove they are better than the ruler they replaced?
- What happens when the person chosen by the old order is not the person people follow?
- How far can a subordinate go before loyalty becomes control?

Then connect the pressure to a relationship, an anomalous action, and a firm but supportable judgment. Do not begin with background, a date, or a lecture topic.

## The causal spine

Write the episode as six answers:

1. Who is under pressure?
2. What do they want to keep or gain?
3. What abnormal event makes that difficult?
4. What choice changes the situation?
5. Who pays the concrete cost?
6. What familiar belief must the audience revise?

If the story cannot answer these, adding more anecdotes will not make it richer.

## Knowledge-to-story conversion

For every necessary fact, write three layers:

1. **Fact:** what the evidence permits.
2. **Situation:** what changed for a named person or group.
3. **Movement:** what became possible, impossible, safer, or more dangerous next.

Example pattern:

> Fact: a successor did not receive expected support.
> Situation: the person prepared to inherit watched delegations go elsewhere.
> Movement: the old ruler's arrangement could no longer decide the outcome.

The situation may only use observable actions supported by sources. Do not invent thoughts, dialogue, weather, crowd size, or precise gestures.

## Openings that hold attention

The first line should be understandable without knowing the historical names. Prefer:

- a universal question plus an abnormal result;
- a familiar story whose common explanation is incomplete;
- a relationship plus an action that should not happen;
- a result first, followed by “how did it reach this point?”

Strong wording is allowed when it is supported. Do not weaken every sentence into vagueness, but do not turn uncertainty into certainty. A hook may withhold an explanation; it may not falsify the event.

## Scene and spoken language

- Write for one breath and one idea at a time.
- Prefer ordinary verbs: leave, block, take, refuse, run, wait, hand over.
- Replace abstract labels with visible consequences: who cannot go home, who must fight, what work stops, what protection disappears.
- Use description when it helps the audience see cause or cost. Do not decorate empty space.
- Keep specialist terms backstage unless the term itself is a familiar anchor; explain it in plain speech when it enters.
- Let the narrator make bounded judgments. A voice with no judgment sounds like notes; a voice with unsupported certainty sounds dishonest.

## Branch-story gate

Add a side story only when all four pass:

1. it is adjacent in time or causality;
2. it has a source and a recognizable anchor;
3. it advances conflict, choice, cost, or interpretation;
4. removing it would make the main story harder to understand or less affecting.

A high-recognition anecdote may appear before the main line when it creates the right question. Vary the entrance: scene, phrase, mistaken memory, object, or consequence. Do not stack anecdotes merely to make the episode longer.

## Ending

End in this order:

1. settle the protagonist's human account: what was kept and what was lost;
2. show the cost to an ordinary participant when relevant;
3. state one portable new understanding in plain language;
4. ask one choice-based question that grows from the cost;
5. optionally open the next causal problem, but do not advertise “the next episode.”

The ending should let a listener retell the episode in one sentence without reducing it to a slogan.
